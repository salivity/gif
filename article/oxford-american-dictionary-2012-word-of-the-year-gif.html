Oxford American Dictionary 2012 Word of the Year GIF

In 2012, the Oxford American Dictionary named "GIF" its Word of the Year, marking the 25th anniversary of the format and celebrating its transformation from a technical computing acronym into a dominant cultural medium. The selection recognized how the term had evolved from an obscure file type into an essential tool for digital expression, journalism, and social commentary.

When Oxford announced "GIF" as the 2012 Word of the Year, it formally defined the term as both a noun and a newly coined verb:

  • Noun: A lossless format for image files that supports both animated and static images.
  • Verb: To create a GIF file of (an image or video sequence, especially relating to an event).

Although Steve Wilhite and his team at CompuServe created the Graphics Interchange Format back in 1987, lexicographers noted that 2012 was the year the word achieved true mainstream ubiquity. The emergence of the verb form was a decisive factor for the dictionary's editorial staff, who track how parts of speech shift and expand in popular usage.

During 2012, events like the London Summer Olympics and the United States presidential election were documented, summarized, and reacted to in real time through looping animations. Oxford lexicographers pointed out that the format had outgrown its reputation as simple internet kitsch, becoming a primary medium for visual humor, news reporting, and emotional nuance on platforms like Tumblr and Twitter. By defining GIF as both the file itself and the act of creating one, the Oxford American Dictionary captured a pivotal moment in the evolution of modern digital language.
